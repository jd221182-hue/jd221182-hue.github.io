Should you drop collision coverage?

On an older car, the most an insurer will pay after a total loss is roughly the car's value minus your deductible. This calculator compares that maximum payout to what you pay each year for collision and comprehensive.

The formula

maximum payout = car value − deductible
cost ratio = yearly premium ÷ maximum payout

With the example numbers, the maximum payout is $4,000 and you pay 15% of that every year. A common personal rule of thumb is to look hard at dropping the coverage once the ratio passes about 10%, but there's no official cutoff.

Before you drop it

  • If there's a loan or lease, the lender almost always requires the coverage.
  • Ask whether you could replace the car tomorrow without hardship.
  • Keep liability and uninsured motorist coverage; those protect you, not the car.
  • Raising the deductible is a middle step that often lowers the cost.

How the ratio changes as a car ages

Car values usually fall faster than collision and comprehensive premiums do, so the cost ratio tends to rise each year. Here's a hypothetical car that's worth $24,000 at age 3 and loses about 15% of its value a year, while the yearly premium for collision and comprehensive falls about 4% a year from $900:

Car age Car value Max payout ($1,000 deductible) Premium Cost ratio
3 $24,000 $23,000 $900 3.9%
6 $14,740 $13,740 $796 5.8%
9 $9,052 $8,052 $704 8.7%
12 $5,559 $4,559 $623 13.7%
15 $3,414 $2,414 $551 22.8%

Separating collision from comprehensive

The calculator combines both because many people think of them as a pair, but they're priced separately and can be dropped separately. On older cars, comprehensive is often cheap relative to the risks it covers: theft, hail, flood, fire, falling trees, and animal strikes. Collision is usually the larger cost. Ask your insurer for each one's price and run the calculator for collision alone.

The self-insurance alternative

If you drop the coverage, put the premium you save into a dedicated savings account. After a few years, that fund often exceeds what the coverage could have paid, and it's yours whether or not you ever crash.

Questions to answer before deciding

  1. Is there a loan or lease? If yes, keep the coverage.
  2. Could you replace the car from savings without hardship?
  3. Does anyone in the household have a higher chance of an at-fault crash (a new driver, for example)?
  4. Is the car worth more locally than pricing guides suggest?
  5. Would a higher deductible get you most of the savings while keeping total-loss protection?
