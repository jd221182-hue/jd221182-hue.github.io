Deductible break-even calculator

A higher deductible lowers your premium. This calculator shows how many claim-free years it takes for the premium savings to cover the extra amount you'd pay if you file a claim.

The formula

break-even years = (higher deductible − current deductible) ÷ yearly savings

With the example numbers: ($1,000 − $500) ÷ $150 ≈ 3.3 years. If you usually go longer than that between claims, the higher deductible saves money over time.

What the math leaves out

  • Cash on hand. Only choose a deductible you could pay tomorrow without borrowing.
  • Small claims. Many people avoid filing small claims anyway because claims can affect future premiums. That makes a higher deductible look better.
  • Percentage deductibles. Some home policies set wind or hurricane deductibles as a percentage of the dwelling limit. A 2% deductible on a $400,000 limit is $8,000. Enter that dollar amount above.

For a longer walkthrough of choosing deductibles on home, auto, and health plans, see how to choose a deductible.

How to use the result

The break-even number answers one question: how many years in a row would you need to go without a claim for the higher deductible to pay for itself? Compare it with how often you actually file claims.

  • Car insurance: many drivers go years between at-fault collision claims. If your break-even is 3 to 4 years and you've had no claims in the last 5, the higher deductible usually wins.
  • Homeowners insurance: homeowners file claims less often than drivers, which tends to favor higher deductibles, as long as you can cover the deductible from savings.
  • Health insurance: use a total-cost comparison instead, because premiums, deductibles, coinsurance, and out-of-pocket maximums all interact.

A 10-year view

Break-even assumes at most one claim. Over a longer period, what matters is the expected number of claims. Here's a hypothetical comparison over 10 years for a car policy where raising the deductible from $500 to $1,000 saves $150 a year:

Claims in 10 years Extra paid in deductibles Premium saved Net result of the higher deductible
0 $0 $1,500 +$1,500
1 $500 $1,500 +$1,000
2 $1,000 $1,500 +$500
3 $1,500 $1,500 $0
4 $2,000 $1,500 −$500

Choosing a deductible you can actually pay

The math only works if the deductible is affordable. A practical rule: your emergency fund should cover your largest deductible, or the two largest if they could plausibly happen in the same year (for example, a storm that damages both your home and your car). If it doesn't yet, start with a lower deductible and raise it as savings grow.

Percentage deductibles

Some home policies, especially in hurricane and hail regions, have deductibles set as a percentage of the dwelling limit:

Dwelling limit 1% deductible 2% deductible 5% deductible
$250,000 $2,500 $5,000 $12,500
$400,000 $4,000 $8,000 $20,000
$600,000 $6,000 $12,000 $30,000

Enter the dollar amount in the calculator. A percentage deductible can be the largest single number in your household's emergency planning.
