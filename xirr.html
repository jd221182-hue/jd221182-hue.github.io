Money-weighted return (XIRR) calculator

If you add money to an account at different times, dividing your gain by your deposits understates or overstates your real return. The money-weighted return (what spreadsheets call XIRR) accounts for when each dollar went in.

The formula

find r so that  Σ CFᵢ ÷ (1 + r)^((dᵢ − d₀)/365) = 0

The calculator solves for r by bisection. With the example flows, $10,000 deposited during the year grows to $10,900. The simple gain is 9%, but the money-weighted annual return is about 13%, because much of the money was invested for only part of the year.

When to use it

  • Checking how your own contributions actually performed.
  • Comparing against a fund's published (time-weighted) return. If you tend to buy after prices rise, your XIRR may be lower.

Very short periods give misleading annual numbers, so use at least several months of data.

Why timing changes the answer

Two investors can deposit the same total and end with the same balance, yet have different money-weighted returns, because the return depends on how long each dollar was invested.

Investor 1 Investor 2
Jan 1 deposit $8,000 $2,000
Oct 1 deposit $2,000 $8,000
Dec 31 value $10,900 $10,900
Simple gain 9% 9%
Money-weighted return about 10.6% about 23.5%

Investor 2 earned the same $900 with most of the money invested for only three months, so the annualized rate is much higher.

Common data mistakes

  • Counting dividends as deposits. Reinvested dividends are part of the return, not new money. Only include money you added from outside the account.
  • Leaving out withdrawals. Money you took out goes in as a positive cash flow on the date you took it.
  • Using the wrong end value. Use the account's market value on the end date, after fees.
  • Mixing accounts. You can compute XIRR across several accounts, but include every deposit and withdrawal from all of them.

Money-weighted vs. time-weighted

A fund's published return is time-weighted: it ignores when investors add or remove money, which makes it fair for judging the fund manager. Your XIRR is money-weighted: it reflects your own timing. If your XIRR is consistently below the funds you own, your deposit and withdrawal timing may be costing you. If it's above, you may have added money at good times.

Checking the calculator

Two quick tests anyone can run:

  • One deposit of −1,000 and one value of 1,070 exactly one year later should return 7.00%.
  • The same pattern over two years with a final value of 1,144.90 should also return 7.00%.
