<p>After a home insurance adjuster inspects the damage, you get an estimate. It's usually a long PDF generated by estimating software, full of codes, abbreviations, unit prices, and columns labeled RCV, ACV, and O&amp;P. Most homeowners skim to the bottom line, compare it with a contractor's quote, and feel confused or shortchanged.</p>
<p>The estimate is negotiable, and the way to negotiate it is to understand it line by line. This guide walks through the anatomy of a typical property claim estimate using a hypothetical bathroom water-damage claim, and lists the most common places estimates come in low.</p>

<h2>The anatomy of an estimate</h2>
<p>Most property estimates follow the same structure, whichever software the adjuster uses:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Header:</strong> claim number, policy number, date of loss, type of loss, adjuster and contact info, and the <strong>price list</strong> used (usually a region code and a month, for example a city code plus "NOV26"). The price list determines unit prices.</li>
<li><strong>Room-by-room sections:</strong> each room has its dimensions (walls, floor, ceiling area, perimeter) and a list of line items.</li>
<li><strong>Line items:</strong> each one has a description, quantity, unit, unit price, tax, overhead and profit (sometimes), RCV, depreciation, and ACV.</li>
<li><strong>Summary page:</strong> totals for RCV, depreciation (split into recoverable and non-recoverable), deductible, prior payments, and the net claim.</li>
</ol>

<ul>
<li><strong>R&amp;R</strong> means remove and replace. Other prefixes: <strong>D</strong> (detach), <strong>R</strong> (reset), <strong>Clean</strong>, <strong>Seal</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Qty and Unit</strong> (SF = square feet, LF = linear feet, EA = each, SY = square yards, HR = hour). This is where measurement errors hide.</li>
<li><strong>Unit price</strong> comes from the price list. It usually includes labor, materials, and equipment for that task.</li>
<li><strong>RCV</strong> is quantity × unit price + tax.</li>
<li><strong>Age/Life and Depreciation:</strong> the adjuster's estimate of the item's age and expected life, and the resulting depreciation. In parentheses means it's being withheld, often as recoverable depreciation.</li>
<li><strong>ACV</strong> is RCV minus depreciation.</li>
</ul>

<p>A gap like this is normal, and much of it is recoverable. The rest of this guide goes through the items where it usually comes from.</p>
<h2>The seven places estimates usually come in low</h2>
<h3>1. Missing overhead and profit (O&amp;P)</h3>
<p>When a job needs a general contractor to coordinate several trades (for example, drywall, painting, flooring, and plumbing), contractors charge for overhead and profit, commonly cited as <strong>10% overhead + 10% profit</strong>. Many estimates leave it out on smaller claims. If your repair genuinely requires coordination of multiple trades, ask for O&amp;P to be included. In the example above, that's the single largest line.</p>
<h3>2. Measurement errors</h3>
<p>Check the room dimensions against your own tape measure. A ceiling that's 9 feet instead of 8 adds about 12% to wall area. Missing a closet, a hallway, or a soffit is common.</p>
<h3>3. Matching and continuous surfaces</h3>
<p>If water ruined the tile in half the bathroom, you often can't buy that tile anymore. Many states' regulations or policy wording require that replaced items match reasonably in quality, color, and size. That can mean replacing the entire continuous surface, not just the damaged squares. The same applies to carpet that runs into a hallway, or paint that has to cover the whole wall to blend.</p>
<h3>4. Paint scope</h3>
<p>Estimates sometimes include only the damaged wall. A painter will typically say the full room needs repainting so that it matches. Also check that the estimate includes primer/sealer coats where stains need blocking.</p>
<h3>5. Detach and reset items</h3>
<p>Toilets, vanities, appliances, baseboards, door casings, and light fixtures often have to be removed and put back to do the work. Each should be a line item. They are easy to miss.</p>
<h3>6. Minimum charges and small trades</h3>
<p>Plumbers, electricians, and tile setters often have minimum charges for a visit. If the estimate prices a 20-minute task at a fraction of a trip charge, ask for the trade minimum.</p>
<h3>7. General conditions</h3>
<p>Permits, floor protection, dumpster or haul-off, final cleaning, and contents moving are real costs that sometimes get left out entirely.</p>

<h2>How to request a revised estimate</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Get your own contractor's bid</strong> in a similar line-item format. Many contractors use the same estimating software and can produce a comparable estimate.</li>
<li><strong>Mark up the adjuster's estimate.</strong> For each line you disagree with, note the change and why: corrected measurement, missing item, O&amp;P, matching.</li>
<li><strong>Send a short written request</strong> to the adjuster with the marked-up estimate, the contractor's bid, and photos. Ask for a revised estimate or a written explanation for each item they won't change.</li>
<li><strong>Ask for a re-inspection</strong> if there are significant differences, and invite your contractor to meet the adjuster on site. Many disputes get resolved in a 30-minute walk-through.</li>
<li><strong>Report hidden damage as a supplement.</strong> If the contractor finds more damage after opening walls, stop and notify the insurer before continuing. Supplemental claims are routine.</li>
</ol>
<h2>If you still can't agree</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Appraisal clause:</strong> most homeowners policies have an appraisal provision for disputes about the <em>amount</em> of loss (not coverage). Each side hires an appraiser and an umpire resolves differences. Read the clause for costs and deadlines.</li>
<li><strong>Your state insurance department</strong> can help if the insurer isn't responding or you believe the claim isn't being handled fairly.</li>
<li><strong>A public adjuster</strong> works for the policyholder for a percentage of the claim. For large or complex claims, that can be worth it; for small ones, the fee may outweigh the gain. Check that they're licensed in your state.</li>
</ul>

<h2>How to mark up the estimate yourself</h2>
<p>You don't need estimating software to make a useful markup. Work through the PDF with three highlighter colors:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Green:</strong> items you agree with.</li>
<li><strong>Yellow:</strong> quantities or measurements you want to check. Write your measurement next to theirs.</li>
<li><strong>Red:</strong> items that are missing or that you dispute. Write a one-line reason and a reference to a photo or bid.</li>
</ol>
<p>Then summarize the red and yellow items in a short table in your email. Adjusters handle dozens of files at once; making the differences easy to see is the fastest way to get them fixed.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<p><strong>Do I have to use the insurer's preferred contractor?</strong>
Generally no. You can usually choose your own contractor, though the insurer may only pay what it considers a reasonable cost.</p>
<p><strong>Why is the unit price so much lower than my contractor's?</strong>
Price lists are based on regional averages. In a busy market, after a regional storm for example, actual local prices may be higher. A contractor's invoices can support a request to adjust.</p>
<p><strong>Is sales tax included?</strong>
Usually, on materials. Check that tax is applied at your local rate.</p>
