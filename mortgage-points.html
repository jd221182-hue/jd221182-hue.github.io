Mortgage points break-even calculator

Discount points let you pay money at closing for a lower interest rate. One point usually costs 1% of the loan amount. The question is how long it takes the lower payment to repay that cost.

The formula

payment = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n)   (r = monthly rate, n = number of payments)
break-even months = cost of points ÷ (payment without points − payment with points)

Example: a $400,000 loan, 6.75% vs. 6.50%, one point ($4,000). The payments are about $2,594.39 and $2,528.27, a difference of $66.12 a month, so break-even is about 60.5 months.

Things that change the answer

  • Selling or refinancing before the break-even means the points cost more than they saved.
  • The cash could have been invested instead; counting that pushes the break-even later.
  • Points may be tax-deductible for some borrowers who itemize. Check IRS guidance for your situation.

A plain-English guide with a chart of break-even months by rate reduction is here: mortgage points break-even.

How long will you keep the loan?

Points only pay off if you keep the loan past the break-even month. Many mortgages end early, through a sale or a refinance, long before 30 years. Be honest about your plans: a move for work, a growing family, or a possible refinance if rates fall all shorten the time you'll actually benefit.

Net savings by how long you keep the loan

Using the calculator's example ($400,000, 6.75% vs. 6.50%, one point for $4,000, saving $66.12 a month):

Years until sale or refinance Payment savings Cost of points Net result
3 $2,380 $4,000 −$1,620
5 $3,967 $4,000 −$33
7 $5,554 $4,000 +$1,554
10 $7,934 $4,000 +$3,934
30 $23,803 $4,000 +$19,803

These figures ignore two more subtle effects. First, with a lower rate, a little more of each payment goes to principal, so the balance falls slightly faster, which helps a bit if you sell. Second, the $4,000 could have been invested or kept as savings instead. If it would have earned 4% a year, the break-even moves later, to about 68 months (roughly five and a half years) in this example.

Points vs. a bigger down payment

The same cash could go toward a larger down payment instead of points. A bigger down payment lowers the loan amount (and the payment) and may help avoid private mortgage insurance if it gets you to 20% down. Compare the monthly savings of each option for the same dollars.

Lender credits: points in reverse

Lenders also offer "negative points," or lender credits: a higher rate in exchange for money toward closing costs. That can make sense if you expect to sell or refinance within a few years, the mirror image of the logic for buying points.

What to ask the lender

  • The exact rate with zero points, one point, and two points, on the same day and the same loan
  • Whether points are quoted as a percentage of the loan amount
  • The annual percentage rate (APR) for each option, which includes points and some fees
  • Whether any of the cost is an origination fee rather than true discount points
